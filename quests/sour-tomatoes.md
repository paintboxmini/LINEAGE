# Sour Tomatoes

*A one-shot encounter in the Fog Basin. No pressure track — the tension here is moral, not mechanical.*

**Session Target:** 1–2 hours. One property, one confrontation, one real choice.

For location overview, see `locations/fog-basin.md`.

---

## Core Concept

The Odom place sits off a fog-line track outside the capital, isolated enough that nobody checks in. Nothing here can be fixed. The father won't reform. The mother is too far gone to help herself, let alone anyone else. The oldest boy already made his choice years before the party arrives.

The only real question the party can answer is what happens to the children who didn't.

---

## The Property

A house and a shed, fifty yards apart, at the end of a track the fog keeps half-erasing. The house is close, dark, cluttered — years of trash nobody was cleaning. The shed is smaller, kept, and lived in by choice, not neglect.

An old pickup sits in the yard when the father and oldest boy are home. **When it's gone, so are they — and it will come back.** That return is the only clock this scene needs. Whatever the party does, they're doing it on borrowed time, not a fixed one.

---

## The Family

**The father.** Cruel by habit, not impulse — he doesn't need a reason. Addicted to whatever's on hand. Reads a room only well enough to find what will hurt worst.

**The mother.** Over five hundred pounds, bed-bound, buried in her own room. She gave up long before the party arrives. She doesn't know how many children she has. She hears something scratching and crying in the ceiling above her bed, screams at it, and pokes a broom handle through the plaster trying to hit it.

**Cole, the oldest.** Takes after his father. Runs errands, keeps secrets, wears the same expression. He is not confused about who he is.

**The middle children.** Somewhere around six of them — the dream this is drawn from wasn't sure, and neither is anyone in the house. They've learned to disappear from their parents and from Cole. Left alone, they play. They are, for the most part, still kids.

**The youngest.** Four years old. Nonverbal. Lives in the crawlspace above her mother's bed. The only sound she makes is the sound the yard cats make when they're hungry, because that's the only language anyone ever responded to. Her mother calls her a cat. So does everyone else, most days, including the middle children, who know better and say it anyway because it's easier. Nobody in the house is certain of her real name.

**Grandma.** Lives alone in the shed, fifty yards off. Not the source of the family's dysfunction — just the only adult on the property who still notices the children exist.

---

## Finding Her

The scratching-and-crying "cat" reads differently the longer anyone actually listens.

- **DC 13 Read** — the rhythm's wrong for an animal. It pauses when it's yelled at. Cats don't wait to see if you're still angry.
- **DC 16 Reason** — the holes the mother's broom has already punched through the plaster are placed like someone's aiming, not swatting blind. She's been at this a while.
- **Fail both** — the party treats it as a cat, same as the family does, until something forces the truth: she cries a real word, or she's found directly.

---

## The Masks

Two clown masks, filthy, live in the truck's glovebox. The father and Cole each pocket one before they leave. What they're for is not established here — an open thread, not a withheld twist. If it matters at your table, decide it once and keep it consistent; it doesn't need resolving for this scene to land.

---

## No Clean Solution — GM Guidance

Do not offer the party a version of this where the father can be reasoned with. He isn't going to change, and nothing in the fiction supports pretending he might.

**Cole fights.** If it comes to violence, he stands with his father, not against him — not out of fear, not because he's been forced to. This is not a rescue the party gets to complete. Let it be a real loss if it happens that way; don't soften it after the fact.

**The mother is not a combat encounter.** She's a hazard to the daughter in the ceiling and an obstacle the party has to get past, not a boss. What happens to her afterward — left, reported, cared for — is a real open question this document doesn't answer for you.

**The actual win condition is the kids, not the parents.** Stopping the father doesn't resolve anything by itself. The measure of how this went is whether the middle children and the youngest end up somewhere safer than where they started — and whether the party got them out before the truck came back, or had to fight their way to that outcome instead.

## Where the Kids Go

Steele's orphanage — rebuilt on the site of the one that burned when he was a child living in it — is a standing answer to exactly this question. See `characters/steele.md` for his history with it. If Steele is in this party or reachable by it, the connection is direct: he built the place that didn't exist for him, and now it's real for someone else. If he isn't present, the orphanage still exists and still takes them — it just costs the party a longer trip to get there.

## Grandma's Treat

Once the truck is gone, Grandma calls the children over: *"Come here — I've got sour tomatoes for you."*

They're cherry tomatoes off her own vine, picked too late, gone soft and split. "Sour" is what she calls spoiled, because spoiled isn't a word you use for a treat you're actually giving someone. It's real food, freely given, and it's the best thing on the property.
