# Economy, Pricing & Trade — Design Trail

## What this trail preserves

This is the historical evolution of Tales Untold's economy: how gold pacing went from a queued concern with no mechanism behind it to a stated ratio rule, how item prices went from mostly ceremonial to a coherent scale, how quest rewards were resolved (including the quests that correctly pay nothing), how the Underground Bazaar's card-trading asymmetry was settled, and how two card-balance concerns (RETALIATE/OVERCOMMIT, SEED) closed out the same day. Almost all of it landed on 2026-08-02, in one long session of Drew answering economy questions the repo had been carrying open for over a week.

The current prices, reward lines, and card texts are authoritative in their own files (`items/`, `quests/`, `cards/`). This file preserves the reasoning trail — why the numbers are what they are, and what got tried and rejected along the way.

## RETALIATE and OVERCOMMIT resolved — and the concern was describing the wrong problem

The queued concern, open since 2026-07-23, was framed as "RETALIATE/OVERCOMMIT/SEED's self-deferred Deadly," with dice or range as the first lever and an extra Deadly stack as the named fallback. Measuring found that framing wrong on both cards it actually named.

RETALIATE needed nothing — the work had already been done and nobody had closed the ticket. Its die had been raised d6 to d8 back on 2026-07-29, and the harness now read it at roughly 51% against a flat Evade reference, with its gate firing on 42% of plays. The named fallback (a third Deadly stack) was measured too and came back slightly worse, ~50.2%, because fights average 4.3 turns and a stack that never gets cashed is a stack wasted. Applying the queued fallback would have been a downgrade.

OVERCOMMIT's problem was never its Deadly, because it has none. As printed it was `Body + d10, Gain Vulnerable` — no Effect, no Defensive Bonus — against STRIKE, the same color, stat, die, and Range, but with a real Defensive Bonus and no self-debuff. OVERCOMMIT was strictly dominated, worse on both axes with no board state preferring it — a dominance argument, not a tuning question, and the sim only needed to measure the cost: 44.1% against STRIKE's 55.9%. The fix added `+1d6` to the Attack line (measuring 50.6%; `+2d6` overshot to 57.5%), keeping the Vulnerable and the absent Defensive Bonus, since the card's identity is offense bought with exposure and it now actually buys some. A harness bug was caught before it reached the report: the first variant test had silently dropped the self-Vulnerable while replacing the damage function, making "as printed" read 49.9% instead of the real 44.1% — the disagreement between two measurements of the same nominal state is what exposed it.

SEED was named in the same old concern but was left alone here — its Deadly ×2 is gated on returning to a planted position, a different mechanism from RETALIATE's, and nothing about it was measured in this pass.

## SEED measured — no change needed

Drew asked for the third card from the same old concern, after RETALIATE and OVERCOMMIT. Same harness: decks identical but for the one card, sides swapped.

Win rate came back fine — 50.8% against STEADFAST, 51.2% against SHADE AWAY, 52.9% against WITNESS in duels, 52.0% in 3v3 with sides swapped. The real finding was that the condition almost never fails: instrumented across turns that started with a live seed, it was at anchor 100% of the time in duels and 99% in 3v3, with roughly 70% of plants cashing overall and the other 30% simply being fights that ended first, not conditions that failed. So the "self-deferred Deadly" framing had overstated the cost — SEED isn't Deadly ×2 at risk, it's Deadly ×2 delayed by one turn, a real cost in tempo but not the gamble the old concern implied. The 100% duel figure wasn't trusted at face value, since duels are already known to be a degenerate corner for position-shaped mechanics (nothing in a 1v1 pressures movement); re-running in 3v3, where forced repositioning actually exists, gave the honest 99% rather than the convenient 100%. The old concern closed fully across all three cards it had named: RETALIATE was already fixed, OVERCOMMIT was strictly dominated and repaired, SEED never needed anything.

## Gold pacing built from scratch

Open since 2026-07-24, the concern had been phrased as wanting "a reference frame for how fast a party should accumulate gear and gold, likely a gold-per-session metric." Reading the repo first showed a number was never the missing piece — the demand side was complete (equipment tiers around 200/800/2,000 gold, roughly fifteen priced consumables, a consistent price convention) against almost no supply side: one gold-loot entry in the entire bestiary, zero quests paying anything, and characters starting with no money by explicit rule.

The rule Drew set, generalized: a session's work is worth about one fifth of the tier the party is currently shopping in. He answered directly — roughly five sessions to a first Tier 1 item — and expressing it as a ratio rather than a flat gold figure lets it hold at every tier, scale with party size without a lookup table, and require a GM to know exactly one thing. It was cross-checked against shipped content before being written down: the Bone Collector's roughly 70-gold hoard works out to about 1.75 early sessions, Senshi's commission rates to about one session, the cheapest consumable to about a third of one.

The same pass surfaced two undefined denominations. Silver had appeared once as undefined loot (`d6+2 silver` from a Session 1 chest) and was nearly recommended for cutting on that basis — but a full Gilded Tusk menu priced six dishes in silver against an eight-gold delicacy, so cutting it would have broken real content or priced dumplings at 0.7 gold. Re-checked against the actual data rather than the first partial grep, the answer became a defined rate: 10 silver = 1 gold. The same sweep then found a third denomination — a menu item priced at 2 copper — and extended the rule to 10 copper = 1 silver = 1 gold, which the whole Gilded Tusk menu then validated on its own: seasoning at 0.02 gold, mains at 0.6–0.9 gold, a limited dish at 1.5 gold, a dangerous-creature delicacy at 8 gold. The rate was confirmed by content that already existed, not asserted from nothing.

Creature loot stayed excluded from the pacing rule by Drew's own call — the Bone Collector's `2d6 × 10` hoard was kept and reframed explicitly as a curator's lair, a place with money in it, not a precedent for gold appearing on ordinary stat blocks. The number itself was written into the rule as a budget rather than a purse: Iron the farmer, whose ground is collapsing, gets paid out three separate ways in his own quest (Fenwool the party has to move, neighbors pooling resources, or a standing debt), with the GM choosing what the table actually earned.

## Prices made coherent across item files

Drew: "all prices were mostly ceremonial... you have full authority to make the prices coherent." The bigger finding was absence rather than error — four of the six `items/` files had no prices at all (`briarwatch-items.md`, `consumables.md`, `vultures-nest-items.md`, and most of `hollow-and-weald-items.md` listed effects and sources with no number anywhere). Only `turnroot-weald-items.md` was fully priced going in, so "make the prices coherent" turned out to mean mostly "make the prices exist."

The pricing scale itself was already latent in shipped content and nobody had written it down: Luminova Leaves heal 4 for about 15 gold, Luminova Powder heals 8 for about 30, and the file already stated the crafting relationship between the two — a clean anchor of roughly **15 gold per single-use point**, using the same 1-point vocabulary `rules/equipment.md` already defined for equipment tiers. Derived from shipped content rather than invented, then written into the pricing file with the anchor named so a later pass can check against it.

Four real incoherences were found and fixed, each with its reason recorded: Sap Vial dropped from 60 to 25 gold, having been strictly dominated by Echothorn Seed at 40 (Echothorn gave more, cost no action, and had no drawback, for less money — the same dominance shape OVERCOMMIT had against STRIKE the same day). Rootstalker Core dropped from 250 to 60, since a single-use item priced above a Tier 1 permanent item (200 gold) inverted what rarity should cost relative to something that works every fight for the rest of the campaign. Vision Shard dropped from 100 to 45 (Scry 3 at roughly three points on the point scale) and Rusted Armor from 80 to 20 (5 temporary HP once, roughly 1.25 points). Root Fibers stayed at 15 gold a strand, but its crafting bonus was clarified in text rather than repriced, since the finished bow it becomes is genuinely Tier 1 equipment while the raw strand is only material.

Seventeen previously unpriced items were given numbers off the same derived scale — 8–15 gold for a single point, 25–30 gold for two points, 45 gold for repeating in-combat value — and `items/fog-basin-items.md` was deliberately left with no gold prices at all, since Pell trades in a memory, a secret, or a name rather than money; that absence was written into the pricing rule itself so a future pass doesn't "fix" it. Full curve after the pass: 8–15 gold single-point, 20–30 gold two-point, 35–50 gold premium and sale rates, 60 gold rare craftable, 150 gold for the one permanent passive item — correctly sitting below Tier 1's 200. Item card sheets were regenerated to match.

## Reward lines applied to all fourteen quests

Drew: "apply reward lines." The queued framing going in was "twelve quests with no reward line," which assumed twelve of them should pay something. Reading them first said otherwise — the actual deliverable became every quest stating what it pays, including the five that pay nothing, each with its reason stated rather than left as silence a GM has to guess about.

`sour-tomatoes.md` pays nothing because its only real question is what happens to the children who are still alive, and a payment answers a different and worse question. `the-wallows-descent.md` pays nothing because nobody hires anyone to go into the Wallows — every objective in it is the party's own (a debt, a dead-end branch, the Warden, a name heard in the echoes) — so there's no client and no fee, though salvage is separately fair game. `settling-stones.md` and `shifting-burrow.md` pay nothing because they're two doors into the same descent, and Iron already pays for the Hollow once; running both doesn't pay twice. The Ashfall arc turned out to have an employer hiding in one sentence of `war-engine.md`'s Core Concept ("They were sent to disable a war machine"), so both Ashfall sessions pay Tier 2, with the reward note carrying the complication the fiction itself creates: the contract was to break an engine, there is no engine, and the party has to report that to whoever holds the purse. `abyssal-ruins-cathedral.md` pays in lair rather than commission, with an explicit line that the stabilizing crystal found there is not loot and must never be priced, since it holds the passage the party crossed to reach it. And `turnroot-weald-adventure.md` pays by the session rather than in a lump, being a framework rather than a single night's work, with most of its payout arriving as harvest — it's the most thoroughly priced region in the world and Senshi buys outright, so a party that treats the forest as a place with things in it out-earns one that waits to be paid, left for the table to discover rather than be told outright. Amounts throughout follow the pacing rule rather than being invented fresh: roughly 20 gold for a single Tier 1 encounter, 40 gold for a session, 160 gold at Tier 2.

## Underground Bazaar card trading resolved

Drew: "the underground bazaar is where card trading happens. it's rare outside of the bazaar." This closed the last open concern in `unresolved-concerns.md`, which had been framed as "the Bazaar has zero mechanical items — a real gap." It was never an items gap — the Bazaar doesn't sell items, it sells cards, and no items file for it was ever coming.

Most of the actual answer was already written across two files nobody had connected: `places/capital/underground-bazaar.md` already described players acquiring experiences worth crystallizing there, with cards representing those experiences, and `world/lineage.md` had already settled that a card's tag "remembers the tradition" it came from. Drew's one sentence completed a thought the repo had already been holding in two halves.

A rule had been misapplied mid-design and it was load-bearing: the argument that trading had to be strictly one-in-one-out rested on "deck size is locked to total stats" — but that rule governs creatures, not players. `rules/cards.md` distinguishes them directly (player decks use the stat-match as a default, enemy decks equal the creature's total stats exactly), and Frost and Steele both grew from 9 to 10 cards after Session 1. Player decks are uncapped. Once that error was caught, it surfaced the real question underneath: Frost and Steele's decks are "all core cards," so a starting party holds nothing but tagless ones — under a tagged-only-trading rule, the Bazaar would have been shut to any party that hadn't already collected tagged cards, at a location sitting directly under the capital rather than at the end of a campaign.

The resolution, Drew's own call, is an asymmetry: sold, but not bought freely. The Bazaar takes anything, because the soul economy it runs on buys experiences and a common one is still yours — that's the same refugee end the location already describes, the queue the tattooist's needle serves. But stock is scarce and almost entirely tagged, because nobody shelves what everyone already knows, so a first-time party can participate immediately by selling. The economic brake was already in the system and needed no new rule: since player decks are uncapped, a bought card is a pure addition, and an eleventh card dilutes every future draw — buying isn't strictly an upgrade, which is what keeps this from becoming a deck-optimization shop without anyone having to legislate it. The best consequence of the design is a fact rather than a mechanic: a bought card's tag doesn't change hands, so a player can walk out holding a Weald-tagged card having never once stood in the Weald — a lineage they carry and can't personally back, which is precisely what the soul economy already is, knowledge separated from the people who earned it.

## Gold pacing ruled per-character, not per-party

Drew asked directly whether the pacing rule was per-character or per-party; the answer, and the rule, is per-character, with the first Tier 1 item arriving at roughly five sessions each. This closed a gap that had been noticed and then shipped anyway: while designing the pacing rule, the arithmetic behind it (40 gold a session party-wide would take a four-person party twenty sessions to gear up) had been worked out and written down as a consideration, but the rule as first drafted said "the party" without ever resolving which reading was meant. Drew had to ask.

Fixed at the source and downstream: `rules/equipment.md` now states per-character explicitly, gives the multiply-by-party-size instruction, and states that a four-person Tier 1 session is worth roughly 160 gold between them. All eight quest reward lines that existed at that point were restated as per-character figures so they scale automatically with party size rather than needing a second pass later. The number stopped being arbitrary once Drew gave the actual session spine — Session 1 ends at the ruins, Session 2 solves the Hollow, Sessions 3–4 are Turnroot, Session 5 is the capital — because five sessions to a first Tier 1 item means gear arrives at the moment the party first stands somewhere that actually sells it. That's now written into the rule as the stated reason for the number five, not just the number itself.

## Seeker's Price and the Tollworn Plate

Drew, describing his own self-playthrough: "in my self playthrough I push a coin into the wood to gain guided passage for awhile." Seeker's Price had already been written as a working toll wall rather than scenery — "these are payments made by travelers for safe passage," with an existing rule that taking anything from it raises Pressure by 1 — but the mechanic had been designed running backwards, built around taking things *out* of the wall rather than the transgressive direction that actually mattered: paying *in*. Drew's own playthrough (pressing in a gold piece, buying guided passage, and having that open the path to the Root Heart) became the source of the mechanic rather than an illustration of one already built.

Two existing rules absorbed almost the whole thing without needing a new subsystem: Navigation already stated that no check is required when following forest signs, and that signs "must be earned, not handed out freely" — paying the wall is how a party earns them. The currency scale set the same day (10 copper = 1 silver = 1 gold) turned out to be load-bearing here too, making copper/silver/gold a meaningful ladder at a wall whose other tolls are pocket change. Three exits now exist and map onto the world's own logic: earn it (three Marks), win it (kill the Heart), or buy it (Paid Passage) — a real rule rather than a session simply wrapping up. Two gaps surfaced during verification and were closed before shipping: the Root Heart's behavior toward a paying party had been unstated, which a GM would reasonably have run as a boss fight, collapsing the third exit back into the second — it now states outright that the Heart does not attack a party that has paid. And a gold piece sitting a foot from a 200-gold Tollworn Plate in the same wall read as an error unless stated plainly: the wall reads the gesture, not the sum, so fifty gold buys exactly what one gold piece buys. Paid Passage stays cheap and returns nothing — no Marks, no kill, no loot, the Heart alive behind the party — deliberately, since empty is the correct trade and an artificial cost would only have been bolted on to make a ledger look balanced.

The Tollworn Plate itself answered a separate instruction: "turnroot session 4 should drop the first piece of armor before the boss fight. 1 piece ahead of time with the party choosing who wears it." It was placed at Seeker's Price because the location had already described itself as the answer — someone wore this armor to the edge of the Weald and decided they wanted through more than they wanted it. The existing Pressure rule became the item's cost and the Mark system its alternative: with at least one Mark, the wood lets go with no Pressure cost, the forest settling accounts with someone who's already paid something; with no Marks, taking it is theft, Pressure +1, one step nearer a forced Root Heart fight. It's plain Armor 1, Tier 1, with no split budget, since it's the first piece of equipment anyone in the campaign will own and should teach the keyword and nothing else. "One only, party picks" was written as a table beat rather than an inventory line, with an explicit out: if the party can't agree, the plate stays in the wall. It deliberately front-runs the gold curve by one session (the first Tier 1 purchase is otherwise paced to arrive at session 5, in the capital), and both files say so, with the item excluded from Turnroot's own income figure so a GM doesn't budget the session around a find rather than a purchase.

## Briarbundles built, and Iron ties the fresh ones

`bestiary/briarbundles.md` (family lore plus four field forms) and `bestiary/the-first-bundle.md` (the boss) filed a five-creature Briarwatch family from Drew's brief: Haywight at 5, Tatterman at 6, Briarbound at 7, Mossking at 10, and the First Bundle at 17, all BRIARWATCH-tagged with no new tag introduced. The connective tissue was already sitting in the location file — Briarwatch's own GM Secret has the Masons building the watchtower and western fence, carving glyphs to hold a line against the Weald, and the Briarbundles are the farmers' folk-magic version of the identical act: built guardians on the same border, except the Masons' work holds through craft and the bundles hold because enough people believed the same thing in the same field for long enough.

A later ruling overturned text written the same day. `quests/the-abandoned-homestead.md` had stated, in bold, "**Do not answer the fresh bundles.** Not this session, not with a note found in the house, not with the Duskwick" — written and meant hours earlier. Drew's direct call, "Iron ties the new bundles," is both legitimate and better: the instruction had been protecting a mystery, and the answer turns out to be characterization rather than a solution. Iron is the farmer still holding his claim together, and the homestead was written as the picture of what his failure looks like — him quietly keeping a dead neighbor's fields tied together is the same restraint his own file already gives him. Drew's decision on how far players can push the reveal was taken as stated rather than assumed further: he confirms it if asked, not sealed and not volunteered, which keeps the house itself still explaining nothing — the encounter's whole point — while the actual answer lives in a different field and has to be walked to. The one loose end this closed off cleanly was the Duskwick living in the abandoned house: Iron works that yard every autumn and never crosses its threshold, because the house isn't his and there's nothing in it he wants, which the encounter had already supported outright. He's now the worked example of the encounter's own safe path. The separate winter thread — old bundles vanishing, the First Bundle growing larger every spring, nobody having connected the two — was deliberately left uncollapsed into this one: Iron answers who ties the *new* bundles and nothing else, and both bestiary files still carry the older, unanswered question.

## Senshi's Bone Collector pricing — closed history, not an open question

An earlier design pass flagged a real-looking discrepancy: Senshi was recorded offering 35 *silver* for a Bone Collector specimen, against his own published commission rates of 35–40 *gold* for creatures far less dangerous to harvest — same buyer, same numeral, a tenfold denomination gap, with the hardest job paying the least. At the time it was flagged rather than fixed, on the reasoning that a price is canon and not something to change on inference — either a unit slip or deliberate lowballing, worth saying out loud rather than silently resolving.

That flag is now closed. Drew confirmed directly, the same day this consolidation pass was written, that it was a unit slip. The live files already carry the fix, both in gold: `places/capital/gilded-tusk.md`'s Senshi's Commission scene quotes 35 gold for a fresh specimen delivered intact; `items/turnroot-weald-items.md` prices Bone Collector Flesh at roughly 40 gold, going rate. Neither carries a silver figure for the specimen anywhere — `gilded-tusk.md`'s only silver-denominated Bone Collector entry is the finished dish (Bone Collector Crisps, 15 silver), a different thing priced in a different currency for a different reason. The tenfold unit-slip Drew confirmed is genuinely gone from both files. What's left is a smaller, different drift — 35 vs. ~40 gold for the same commission, both inside the 35–40 range that made the original discrepancy legible as a slip in the first place, but not identical to each other. That's logged separately in `unresolved-concerns.md` as ordinary uncaught price drift (the same shape `check_hp_formula` catches for the HP formula, with no equivalent check for item prices yet) — not reopening the question Drew already answered, just naming what's actually still there.

## Durable lessons

- A queued concern should be re-measured before being fixed, not assumed correct as originally framed — two of the three cards named in the RETALIATE/OVERCOMMIT/SEED concern didn't have the problem the concern described.
- A pacing rule expressed as a ratio (a session buys roughly a fifth of the current shopping tier) survives party-size and tier changes that a flat gold figure would need a lookup table to handle.
- An undefined denomination hiding in already-shipped content (silver, then copper) is a rate waiting to be confirmed by what's already there, not invented from nothing.
- A reward line that states "nothing, and here is why" closes a gap more honestly than a reward line that states a number nobody thought hard about.
- An asymmetric rule (sold freely, bought scarcely) can resolve a two-sided design tension without inventing a new mechanical brake, when an existing property of the system (uncapped, diluting deck size) already supplies one.
- A flagged discrepancy that reads as a possible bug is worth recording as flagged rather than silently "fixed" on inference — and worth explicitly closing, not just leaving to age, once the live files and Drew's own word confirm which reading was correct.
