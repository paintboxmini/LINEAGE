# Initiative Wheel — Design Trail — EXEMPLAR

> **Exemplar:** This consolidated design trail is the reference example for how historical design trails should be preserved in `archives/consolidated/`.

## What this trail preserves

This is the historical design trail for LINEAGE's initiative wheel: how turn order evolved from a conventional ordered sequence into a continuous positional structure, where the difficult cases came from, and which distinctions ultimately mattered.

This is historical context, not the current rules authority. Current initiative rules live in the canonical combat and glossary files.

## The original model

Initiative began as an ordinary ordered turn sequence: participants rolled initiative, were placed in order, and acted highest to lowest.

The design then moved toward a **continuous wheel**. The important conceptual change was that turn order stopped being a list that reset after the last participant. The participants occupy a closed loop, with a marker representing where the present moment is. The marker advances from token to token, and the structure persists rather than resetting into a new round.

The wheel created a useful design space: effects could move a participant through the order itself. Initiative was no longer merely a value rolled at the beginning of combat; it became a manipulable part of the battlefield.

## Why a wheel instead of a list

The wheel made initiative positional and persistent. A participant could be shifted forward or backward relative to the marker, allowing cards to manipulate tempo without introducing a second initiative system.

The cost was that a closed loop has no natural beginning or end. A list can say "later" by moving toward its tail. A wheel needs a reference point to distinguish the next turn from a complete revolution back to the same place.

That problem became the **fence / turn marker** problem.

The marker is not a person. It is the reference point that tells the system which visit to a position is the present one. This distinction became important because a token's physical position and its scheduling/count relationship could diverge.

## The hard problem: Initiative Shift X

Initiative Shift X was introduced as the mechanism for manipulating wheel position. Positive and negative shifts needed strong invariants:

- a positive shift must never make the target wait longer for its next turn;
- a negative shift must never make the target act sooner than the normal progression allows.

The obvious arithmetic became increasingly ugly at large shifts. On a wheel with `N` combatants, a shift of magnitude `N` or greater can wrap through one or more complete revolutions. The earlier model therefore accumulated special handling for full laps, bonus turns, skipped turns, and interactions with the marker.

This was the point where the wheel's elegance began producing complicated cyclic-permutation math.

## Rejected direction: replace the wheel with a linear track

One serious alternative was to abandon the wheel entirely and use a bounded linear track whose length matched the number of combatants.

The proposed model gave the track explicit leading and trailing edges. Participants acted from the lowest occupied slot, then moved toward the back. A shift could be clamped against an edge instead of wrapping around a closed loop. An early version also considered stacking pieces on the same slot, resolved last-in-first-out.

This direction was not rejected because it failed mechanically. It exposed something useful: much of the apparent complexity was being generated by the wheel's lack of edges and by the need for a fixed reference point.

The deeper diagnosis was that replacing the entire wheel to solve one boundary condition would be solving too much.

Piece stacking was subsequently rejected as unnecessary.

## The smaller diagnosis: count and slot are different layers

The most important conceptual discovery in this trail was that **count and slot are not the same thing**.

A slot is a spatial position on the wheel. Count is the scheduling information that determines when a participant acts. The marker moves through slots, but a participant's actual turn can depend on how its count relates to the marker's current visit.

This explained why several attempted fixes felt wrong. Efforts to protect the marker's slot, prevent sliding through it, or add special cases for every way a token could reach the marker were treating a scheduling problem as though it were a spatial problem.

Sliding changes the arrangement of slots; it does not by itself change the underlying count. The marker's interaction with count is therefore the layer that needs to explain pass-overs and bonus/skip behavior.

That distinction became more valuable than any individual patch.

## The current historical direction

The working direction was to **keep the wheel and remove the need for large wraparound arithmetic by imposing a boundary condition on shifts**: a shift should not be allowed to move a target's effective scheduling position past the marker in the direction that would violate its invariant.

A representative example used four combatants with the marker at slot 1. A target at count 2 shifted negatively by 2 would naively land at count 4, which would represent a full lap and therefore an immediate return to the actor. Under the clamp model, the shift instead stops at the position immediately before the marker's next visit.

The attraction of this approach was that it removed the need to count complete revolutions merely to preserve the invariant. The wheel could remain the underlying structure.

However, this direction was **not yet treated as final canon in the historical working document**. Ordinary non-wraparound pass-overs still needed full re-verification, terminology around count and slot remained under examination, and the canonical glossary/combat wording had not yet been rewritten at the time of the archived discussion.

The lesson is therefore more durable than the provisional implementation: **when a cyclic initiative model becomes difficult, distinguish the spatial layer (slot and sliding) from the scheduling layer (count and the marker) before adding exceptions.**

## Why this mattered to the broader system

The wheel changed initiative from a passive ordering stat into a manipulable combat axis. Cards such as Initiative Shift, Slipstream, and Synchrony could make position in the order itself part of deck identity.

That made initiative a build-around surface rather than merely a tie-breaker or opening roll. It also meant that the underlying model had to remain legible enough that card effects could manipulate it without requiring players or designers to perform hidden cyclic arithmetic.

The historical pressure on the system was therefore not simply "make Initiative Shift work." It was:

> Preserve a continuous, manipulable initiative structure while making the relationship between position, scheduling, and the turn marker understandable enough to support card design.

## What was learned

1. **A wheel creates a reference-point problem that a linear list does not.**
2. **The marker is a structural reference, not a participant.**
3. **Sliding and shifting are different operations.** Sliding rearranges neighboring tokens; shifting changes a target's initiative relationship.
4. **Slot and count are distinct concepts.** Confusing them produces patches that solve the wrong layer.
5. **Large cyclic shifts are a symptom worth diagnosing, not an invitation to pile on more wraparound exceptions.**
6. **The wheel is valuable enough to preserve if its invariants can be expressed without exposing its underlying arithmetic to play.**
