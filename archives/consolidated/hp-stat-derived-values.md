# HP and Stat-Derived Values — Design Trail

## What this trail preserves

This is the historical design trail for how Tales Untold's derived values — HP, hand size, initiative, movement, and difficulty rating — came to be *derived* at all, rather than independent numbers a designer sets by feel. It traces the HP formula through three real changes (not one), the renaming and formalization of the difficulty scale, the hand-size floor's own back-and-forth, and the Wound rules that wire a temporary stat change into a temporary derived-value change.

This is historical context, not the current rules authority. The formulas and values actually in effect live in `rules/core-rules.md` and `rules/card-glossary.md`; this file exists to explain why they ended up shaped the way they did, not to restate them as if this were their source.

## The founding principle: derived values follow the stat, always

Before any formula existed, the underlying rule was decided: **a stat's derived value moves when the stat moves — permanently or temporarily, up or down.** Body governs max HP. Mind governs hand size. Soul governs initiative *and* movement speed — high-Soul characters move faster and act first, which is why flee and chase checks use Soul, not Body ("Body is impact, not velocity"). Only Body touches HP; Mind and Soul don't.

This became a general rule, not a keyword: any card that reduces a stat for the fight (Sunder → Mind, Wither → Body, Erode → Soul) lowers whatever that stat governs for the same duration, and reverts at combat's end. Body loss costs 3 max HP per point lost (clamping current HP, capable of forcing a Collapse); Mind loss shrinks hand size; Soul loss slows initiative. Every stat-draining card, present and future, inherits this without needing its own bespoke wording. The combat simulator's `Combatant.erode` implements the Body-only HP case as the concrete proof the rule actually behaves this way, not just reads that way.

The same wiring later carried the Wound rules (see below): a Wound is fundamentally a hand-slot occupied, and the whole apparatus around it — how it's removed, what counts as a debuff, what a short rest can and can't reach — was built on the same "derived value tracks the underlying state" logic as stat loss.

## The HP formula's long arc

The formula that turns Body into HP was rewritten three separate times over the course of the campaign's balance work — not once, and not in a straight line. Each version was a real, dated ruling, and each was tested against the same yardstick: the Frost/Steele matchup, the one pair of decks built by real players rather than assembled for the sim.

**Version 1 — (3 × Body) + 6.** The original baseline. Simple, and it treated Body as doing exactly one job in HP terms: flat toughness on top of a flat base.

**Version 2 — (2 × Body) + 9.** Changed via a sim experiment aimed at Steele's dominance over Frost (~66% at the time). The diagnosis was Body's double-dip: Body drives red damage *and* HP under the old formula, so a high-Body deck paid for survivability and offense with the same stat point. The flatter curve decoupled the two — it crossed over at Body 3 (both formulas give 15 there, so the player baseline didn't move), trimmed HP at high Body (Body 4: 18→17; Body 8: 25 flat under the old formula, meaningfully less under the new one), and lifted it at low Body (Body 1: 11; Body 2: 13). Existing creature stat blocks kept their originally authored HP under a GM-set-HP allowance; new content and players used the new formula.

The sim's own finding undercut the premise almost immediately: **HP was not the main lever for Steele's dominance.** Even flattening HP entirely left Steele winning 59% against Frost — the real driver was red damage plus color spread, not toughness. A parallel damage-dice rebalance (Pain is Fuel and Push d6→d4, Stillness and Excavate d2→d4, Attune d2→d4) narrowed the color EV gap (RED 2.62→2.53, BLUE 2.15→2.21, GREEN 2.44→2.47) but likewise didn't move deck-level balance — Steele barely budged (66% vs Frost). The conclusion drawn at the time: damage is stat-plus-die, and a flat +4 from a Body-4 stat dominates a one-step die change; single-card dice tweaks are the right tool for Oracle-draft attractiveness, the wrong tool for reining in a high-stat deck. A wider sweep (game-length testing across dice-up/dice-down variants) concluded separately that **game length was the actual master balance variable** — bigger dice shorten fights and let raw stats convert to wins more directly, while longer fights give RPS reads, card advantage, and defense more room to matter. HP-flattening helped only a little for exactly this reason: it barely moved game length.

**Version 3 — back to (3 × Body) + 6.** A later, explicit reversion, flagged as one before it was built: *"max HP= 3xBody + 6."* This directly contradicted the still-standing Version 2 ruling, and was named as a reversion rather than silently applied. The concern about Body's double-dip was real and re-measured — Frost (Body 3) vs. Steele (Body 4) across 20,000 paired seeds went from 62.2%/35.8% under the old (Version 2) formula to 59.3%/38.9% under the reverted one, a genuine +3.1-point swing toward the higher-Body deck. But the *direction* of the problem had flipped since Version 2 was written: by this point Steele was losing at 35.8%, not winning at 66%, because later damage and range work had overshot the original correction. The reversion moved the matchup toward even, not away from it — the original double-dip concern was mechanically correct and directionally wrong for the numbers as they now stood. The rescale touched only the tails (Body 3 stayed at 15 either way; Body 1 dropped 11→9; Body 8 rose 25→30), which meant a genuine sweep for any content whose thresholds depended on old HP numbers — `bestiary/the-first-bundle.md`'s quarter-HP phase thresholds moved and the arithmetic finally divided evenly; `bestiary/skeinwing.md` had a stale restated HP figure; the Minotaur's HP happened to land exactly on the new formula, so its bespoke-boss marker was removed because it was no longer bespoke. Absolute low-HP thresholds (jackalopes flee at 1 HP, Borrowers burrow at 3, the Bone Collector retreats at 5) were deliberately left untouched — those read as "nearly dead," not as fractions of max HP, so the reversion didn't ripple into them.

**Version 4 — (3 × Body) + Soul + Mind.** A later formula change superseded Version 3 entirely. This is the formula currently in effect (`rules/core-rules.md`); the Version-3 reversion's own record notes explicitly that it is "doubly superseded" — kept as a record of the Body-double-dip reasoning and the sweep discipline it modeled, not as a description of any formula still live. This file does the same: the current formula lives in `rules/core-rules.md` and `CLAUDE.md`, not here.

## "Strength" became "Creature Threat Rating," and three formal rules replaced difficulty tiers

Difficulty for creatures originally lived on the tiers Early/Mid/Late. That was replaced by a single number: the sum of a combatant's three stats, first called **Strength**. The unification insight was that players already obeyed this rule without it being written down — trifecta spreads already summed to 9, player decks already ran 9 cards with color counts matching stats, and player HP was already formula-derived. The ruling made one law out of two customs rather than inventing something new: **HP by formula, deck size equal to total stats (color counts equal to each stat, signature cards counting toward their color), and difficulty equal to total stats** — precise and self-documenting against the player baseline of 9.

The term itself didn't survive contact with genre convention. "Strength" collided with the Body stat and read as physical power to anyone parsing it cold — the same overload-and-silent-misreading failure mode the repo had already caught twice elsewhere (invariants vs. design principles; exemplars vs. the compiled crib). It was renamed to **Creature Threat Rating** — a straight swap chosen specifically because it introduced no new abbreviation and collided with nothing else in the system. The rename touched every stat-block file's `**Strength:** N` line and every prose mention that used it as the mechanical term, while ordinary-English uses of "strength" (flavor text, "recombine strengths" in the card-compression tool) were deliberately left alone. Past entries that used "Strength" as the then-current canonical term were left as originally written, per this archive's own convention of not rewriting history.

**Boss HP bespoke exception.** In the same window, a separate ruling settled whether every creature — including bosses — had to take formula HP as printed. The answer: **boss HP is bespoke; the formula is the baseline a generator defaults to, not a ceiling.** Root Heart was restored from a flattened 19 back to 30 (its own Pulse thresholds at 20 HP / 10 HP only read correctly against 30 — the bespoke number wasn't arbitrary, a uniform sim-driven pass had simply been applied without knowing that), and the Minotaur was restored from 21 to 24. Both were marked explicitly with a bespoke-exception note so the departure from formula would never be silent — the same anti-drift discipline the Canon Gate applies to Authority-2 extensions, in miniature. A same-day ruling under the Creature Threat Rating rename briefly stated the opposite ("no bespoke HP, always formula") before the bespoke-boss allowance was reaffirmed — the tiers-to-CTR unification lasted about six hours before the bespoke case reasserted itself, which is the gate working as intended: a cheap, reversible ruling surfaced the real answer fast. The bespoke-boss allowance, marked and explicit, is what's live today (`CLAUDE.md`, Stat Blocks).

## Hand size: three positions, ending in a floor

Hand size is Mind's derived value, and it went through the same kind of back-and-forth as HP did, driven by the same instrument — the sim couldn't actually test the question until team combat existed.

**Position 1 — Mind + 1.** The original rule, treated as a load-bearing defensive floor: Mind 2 gave a hand of 3, meaning a combatant could attack and still hold two blocks; nobody was ever fully defenseless.

**Position 2 — drop the +1, tabled.** Testing showed dropping the floor made Mind matter far more as a stat (a Mind-4 blue deck went from 69% to 80% against Steele), but the reasoning for why was structural: hand size is really *blocking capacity between turns*, which only shows up when more than one attacker can hit you before your next turn — something the 1v1 duel simulator structurally could not measure. Removing the floor risked making Mind 2 focus-fire bait and Mind 4 a dominant wall, but there was no team sim yet to check it against, so the change was tabled rather than shipped.

**Position 3 — Mind, no floor, ruled once the team sim existed.** With team combat built, Drew ruled hand size = Mind outright, reasoning that a 1-block-by-default character compensating through Resist, card draw, and ally support "is the system working" rather than a gap to patch — plus character progression and equipment exist as compensating layers the sim doesn't model and arguably shouldn't. The evidence backed the ruling: duel and team win rates both narrowed toward even, fights ran roughly 15% faster everywhere, and the specific "wall" fear (Mind-4 Sage vs. Mind-2 Steele) dropped from 88.9% to 60.1% once earlier blue/scry nerfs were accounted for — no dominant wall materialized.

**Position 4 — Mind, minimum 2.** The encounter-generation stress test surfaced a systemic side effect nobody had chosen on purpose: every Mind-1 creature (Scratcher, Jackalope, Borrower Sentry) now held exactly 1 card, meaning it was fully defenseless after acting — a de facto rebalance of every piece of existing Early-tier content, not an intended texture change. Drew's fix was the simplest one that produced the desired behavior: **hand size = Mind, with a floor of 2.** This restored Mind-1 creatures' pre-change behavior (act, still hold one block), closed the theoretical Mind-0 total-shutdown edge case, and had zero measurable effect on any player-facing deck, since every roster character already ran Mind 2 or higher. One piece of existing content had been built directly on the now-closed Mind-1 defenselessness window — the Fencerow Shrike's vulnerability design — and was reworked from an emergent hand-economy gap into a designed commitment cost (its dive card gained an explicit "you cannot defend until your next turn" clause) rather than left to rely on a mechanic that no longer existed.

The formula in effect today — **hand size = Mind, minimum 2** — is stated in `rules/core-rules.md` and `rules/card-glossary.md` (Stat Change); this file traces how it got there, not what it currently says.

## The Wound rule and stat-derived values wired together

Wounds were formalized around the same "derived value tracks state" logic as ordinary stat loss, but as a status card rather than a stat penalty: a Wound cannot be played, sits in hand occupying a slot, and does not leave on its own. It's removed in combat by spending an action to destroy one from hand, or via short rest (destroying it from wherever it's sitting), or fully cleared on a long rest. Debuff scope was drawn precisely at the same time: **stat reduction and status-card infliction count as debuffs** (which Ward can block), while **discard effects and scrying your own deck do not** (Ward doesn't apply; Obscure is the answer to those instead).

Drew's later reasoning for letting Wound removal work identically outside of combat ran through the fiction, not the mechanics: if a player can spend a combat action to patch one Wound mid-fight, there's no principled reason the same single action can't happen while walking down a road — *"while I'm walking I tear a piece of fabric and wrap my arm... I can't stop them as the DM, right?"* The distinction he drew instead was scale: clearing **several** Wounds back-to-back outside combat is a different, bigger ask, and that's legitimately where requiring a short rest holds up — short rests already chain, so clearing a stack of Wounds already costs *time* (one rest per Wound) rather than needing a separate bigger-single-rest number. The actual rule change was narrow: the action-based single-Wound removal isn't gated behind "spend your official combat action" outside of combat, it's just an ordinary fictional beat there, still capped at one Wound per use either way.

A related, earlier fix removed a self-inflicted cost that had made the stat-drain cards actively punishing: Sunder (Mind), Erode (Soul), and Wither (Body) originally shuffled a Wound into the user's own deck as a cost, which proved crippling once Wounds stopped auto-discarding and started sitting persistently in hand. Removing that self-cost was sim-confirmed to matter — Mire's win rate against Frost jumped from roughly 23% to 59.7%, closing what had been a real, mechanically-imposed trap on using the stat-drain kit at all.

## What was learned

1. **A derived value should always follow its governing stat** — temporarily, permanently, up or down — rather than being treated as an independently-set number. This is the rule every other entry in this trail is a consequence of.
2. **A single-format sim result can be directionally correct about a mechanism and still wrong about which way to turn the dial** — the HP double-dip diagnosis was right both times it was raised, and still produced a formula change in opposite directions twice, because the surrounding balance had moved in between.
3. **An overloaded term degrades reasoning even when its mechanical definition is precise.** "Strength" was correct as a formula; it was still wrong as a name, because it collided with Body hard enough to invite misreading.
4. **A number that looks like a floor is really a claim about who compensates for its absence.** Hand size's three-position history is really an argument about whether Resist/draw/support/progression/equipment are allowed to carry a low-Mind character, settled only once the instrument (team sim) existed to check the claim.
5. **An exception is safe exactly to the degree it's labeled.** Bespoke boss HP survived because every instance carries an explicit marker naming the formula it departs from and by how much — the same discipline that makes any Authority-2 extension reversible.
