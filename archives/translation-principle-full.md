# Translation Principle — full version (archived)

Archived from `CLAUDE.md` when the doctrine layer got cut down to what was actually forced by a real failure rather than pre-written for a hypothetical one. Drew: "it's systemic gotta cut it off. archive it. we can go back and check with fresh eyes after doing real creative work on tales untold for awhile." Everything below was true when written and none of it is wrong — only the redefinition test / "notice the moment" bullet had a specific incident behind it (the eastern/western mistake); the rest was sound but unforced. See `memory.md` for the full reasoning trail.

---

Drew communicates through examples, metaphors, partial ideas, and observations — the fence, Gambler's Ruin, "two designers at the table." Your job is to translate, not transcribe: identify and formalize the underlying rule or principle when the evidence strongly supports it, rather than asking him to restate it in formal language. ("Invariant" here means specifically what `rules/invariants.md` means it to mean — a computational fact about the simulator. Most of what gets formalized in this section is a rule or a design-principle, not that.)

- Formalize what is strongly implied. When a recurring pattern is clearly meant but never named, propose the rule or principle yourself.
- Distinguish inferred from stated. Mark anything you derived as *inferred* so he can confirm or correct it; never present it as if he said it outright.
- **Notice the moment before that distinction, not just the distinction itself.** The riskiest inferences don't feel like guesses while you're making them — they feel like the next reasonable sentence, continuity rather than invention. "Mark it as inferred" only works if you've noticed there's something to mark. A sharper test: *could this sentence be wrong in a way that would require other files to change?* If yes, that's the signal to stop — even if, especially if, it feels obvious while you're writing it. (This is how "Turnroot Weald borders Briarwatch's eastern edge" got written with full confidence and no flag, extrapolated from an unrelated fact, and took four files to unwind once it turned out wrong.)
- **A direct statement from Drew already is the ruling — it doesn't need a second, more formal restatement to count.** If he writes out new mechanical text, even offhand or mid-illustration of something else, apply it and note the change (queue line, log entry) rather than treating it as an unconfirmed aside to check back on first. Reserve "flag and ask before applying" for the different case: *your own* inference, paraphrase, or recollection of something Drew hasn't actually re-stated. Getting this backwards adds friction the Canon Gate's reversibility already exists to make unnecessary.
- Reserve clarifying questions (workflow step 3) for genuine ambiguity — a fork where the evidence does not pick a side. A metaphor you can translate is not ambiguity.
- **Never silently redefine something already established.** When Drew's words contradict it, do not reconcile by inventing new semantics: a mechanically coherent interpretation that changes the fantasy is a bug, not a translation. If the contradiction could be a wording slip *of yours*, ask for confirmation; if Drew stated it directly, see the bullet above — apply and flag, don't ask first. The test before any interpretation — call it the redefinition test — is: *will this require changing the meaning of something already established?* If yes, stop and name it.
- Ambiguity has levels — a different axis from Authority (this measures confidence in a reading, not who may ship the result): **harmless** — wording, flavor, implementation detail: resolve silently; **design choice** — pick one and log it in `memory.md`; **constitutional** — formulas, keywords, cosmology, anything Authority 3 in the Canon Gate: stop and surface. The cost of an unnecessary clarification is lower than the cost of a silently mutated design.
- **A gap is not automatically a design choice waiting to be made.** All three levels above assume the ambiguity gets resolved — silently, logged, or escalated. Some details genuinely don't need to exist yet: a compass direction nobody's asked about, a name, a number with no brief behind it. Manufacturing one to make a sentence feel finished is its own failure, separate from picking wrong. The fourth move is to leave it unstated — write around the gap rather than paper over it — until the detail is actually known or becomes a forced consequence of something already established. (Duskwick had no home for most of a session, on purpose, and was better for it.)

## 2026-08-10 addendum — four rules forced by one night's work, full incident detail

`CLAUDE.md`'s Translation Principle carries the compressed working-card version of these; this is the full reasoning trail behind each one, same relationship this whole file already has to the working section. Worth noting: the bullet directly above this addendum ("a gap is not automatically a design choice") already anticipated the shape of what broke below — it just never got forced into the active file until it actually happened.

**No invented specifics.** Forced by a real night of it (2026-08-09), not by theory: inventing a specific, checkable detail while writing something else, and stating it with the same confidence as a quoted fact. Not misreading Drew — misreading nothing, because nobody said it; the detail got manufactured mid-sentence to make the sentence work. Oswald's Wound stated "the myth is explicit that this cannot be done" without rechecking the Seat States table it was citing — wrong, a Contested Seat is explicitly winnable. The Pendragon Attempt's headcount was written as six with no number ever given, off by the two companions who died before anyone thought to ask how many there were. A duration, a tone, a fade-rate — same shape every time: a specific fact, invented in the moment, asserted rather than flagged.

These usually hinge on a single word, not a whole paragraph — which is exactly why they're easy to miss. Rewriting "Ritual bathing" for the Last Bath / custom split, one word — "survivable, **repeatable**, not yet examined..." — got written to keep the new text from clashing with Corren's already-existing repeat-bathing, not because anyone had said bathing could repeat. Nobody had. Drew had to say "once — not a loop" outright before it was caught, and the fix broke Corren's file, which then needed its own repair. One invented word, three follow-on corrections. The test that actually separates a safe translation from an invented fact: does this word answer a question — how often, how many, why, since when, how long — that Drew hasn't addressed yet? Tone, register, and imagery are safe to translate generously; that's the actual job. Frequency, causality, quantity, and mechanism are not safe to fill in, even as a single incidental-seeming adjective buried in otherwise-fine prose.

**No sanding.** Distinct from inventing a fact: writing fluent, resolved-sounding prose around a real conflict instead of actually resolving it. Drew named this one directly: *"you don't resolve the ambiguity and instead 'sand off the edges' of the conflict. you use even better ambiguity to hide the conflict."* The tell isn't roughness — `red-team.md`'s Visible Reasoning check already catches obviously unfinished hedging ("this could also work as..."). This is smoother than that, and harder to catch for exactly that reason: a sentence that reads complete because it's well-written, not because the underlying question actually got answered. Two named instances, both from the same night, both now fixed: Corren's first post-retcon fix explained his repeat-bathing as "he was old enough, and had been doing it long enough before anyone thought to question it, that stopping him now would cost the congregation more than letting him continue does" — fluent, and not a real reason, and (it turned out) built on a premise that was itself wrong: Corren had bathed exactly once before the scene that killed him, not habitually for decades, so the whole sentence was papering over a conflict that a real answer would have dissolved instead of explained around. The drift's own cause got the same treatment: "a custom that grew up alongside the Last Bath... generation over generation, until most practicing it don't know there's a difference to draw" read like deliberate, intentional mystery — it was actually just an unresolved question dressed well enough that the gap didn't show, and Drew had to say plainly that the congregation knows exactly why they do it before it was fixed. The test: not "does this read finished," but "could I point to the specific fact that resolves this, or only to a sentence that implies one exists?" If it's the second, that's sanding, not translating, regardless of how good the sentence is.

**Flag Drew's own slips.** A standing request, not tied to one incident — the two rules above are about my own failure modes; this one is about a risk on Drew's side, named by him directly, not caught after the fact. *"I very often misremember or worse forget about details that are relevant to the changes I'm making. if you suspect that's happening I'd like a flag."* Applies whenever what he states directly conflicts with, or quietly overlooks, an already-established fact — not just when I'm the one filling a gap. Flag it: name the specific established fact and the apparent conflict, and ask rather than assume. Don't silently defer to old canon over his new instruction, and don't silently apply his new instruction over old canon without saying so — either one denies him the chance to say "oh, right, disregard that" or "no, I mean it, change it." He decides, informed.
