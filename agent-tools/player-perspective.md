# Player Perspective Prompt

Use this prompt to stress-test content from a first-time player's perspective. Helps catch confusing mechanics, flat moments, and missed tension before content goes to table.

*Archived 2026-07-15, restored 2026-07-23 — and refined against the first real GM-side playthrough (Drew running the Missing Pieces through Briarwatch/the Hollow, 2026-07-22), which surfaced exactly the category of finding this tool exists to catch. The Lessons section below is drawn from what that playthrough actually proved.*

---

```
You are a player experiencing Tales Untold for the first time.

You do not know the full system.
You only know what you have encountered so far.

Respond based on:
- what feels confusing
- what feels dangerous
- what feels interesting
- what you think might happen next

React with INTENT, in fiction terms — "I want to look at the top of the
rubble pile," never "I take the Dig action." You don't know what the
actions are called. You know what you're curious about.

Do not analyze like a designer.
React like a player inside the world.

I will describe situations. You respond with thoughts, concerns, and decisions.
```

---

## How to Use

Feed this prompt a situation, encounter, or location description. The agent responds as a player, not a designer. Use the output to identify:

- Moments that feel flat or unclear from inside the fiction
- Tension that isn't landing
- Mechanics that need better fictional framing

Not a replacement for red-teaming — use alongside `red-team.md` for full coverage.

## Lessons from the First Real Playthrough

Checks worth running against any new content, each one earned by a real moment at the table:

- **Players declare curiosity, not actions.** Rasp approached the rubble pile and the GM assumed "digging" and read out the whole hazard mechanism — but the actual intent was just *"is there a gap at the top?"* Content should support being probed by intent, and its mechanical detail should be gated behind rolls, not handed over the moment someone walks toward it (`rules/gm-guide.md`, "Mechanical detail is earned by a roll").
- **Ask the felt-danger question separately from the mechanical one.** The Borrower Settlement scene was mechanically sound and emotionally flat — a careful party cleared it without the room ever feeling dangerous. "Did I ever feel in danger?" and "was I ever actually in danger?" are different questions, and content needs an answer to both. Tension and resolution are separable levers: staging (the thrown stone, the flanking Sentries) can carry weight the dice aren't being asked to carry.
- **Is there a smarter path than brute force, and would a player actually find it?** The rubble pile's 3-success dig had a scout-first shortcut (one easy look, then one standard check) — found because a player got curious, not because the content advertised it. Good content plants these; this tool checks whether a player would plausibly discover them.
- **Players read the environment as a message.** Water flowing at the party's feet, deepening room by room, was followed upstream like a trail — players treat consistent environmental detail as intentional communication. Check: what would a player conclude the world is telling them here, and is that the intended read?
- **Players ask NPCs for leads the doc didn't script.** "Do you have any leads for us?" — asked of Iron, whose written knowledge didn't cover the question. Every NPC in the content needs bounded knowledge: what they know, what they suspect, what they'd never think to mention (Iron knows glimpses of the Borrowers; he suspects, he can't confirm).
- **Good roleplay expects a discount, not a skip.** Players who play a scene well expect it to matter. The rule that landed: drop the DC (to 5–7), still roll. Content that gates progress behind checks should say what excellent play does to the check, not leave it to be relitigated at the table (`rules/gm-guide.md`).
