# Finding a Voice

*Developed from a Drew/GPT brainstorm, 2026-08-07, after a repo-wide scan found the actual gap: locations get built as lenses — one governing question refracted through architecture and custom — but named people mostly get built as functions, a name attached to a role. This file is the same discipline pointed at a person instead of a place. Checked by `agent-tools/alignment-checker.md`'s "Stuck on an NPC" pass; read this first for the full method, that file for the compressed reminder.*

---

## The Test

**Does this person's want predate and outlast the party's reason for meeting them?**

A function exists because the party needs something from it — an item, an explanation, a quest. A voice exists whether or not the party ever shows up. If everything true about a character stops being true the moment the scene they're introduced in ends, they're a function, however well the function is dressed.

This isn't a defect to eliminate everywhere. Functions are honest and often correct — a door guard is a door guard. The test is for *named* people the story leans on more than once, or wants the party to remember. Not every stall in a marketplace needs a soul. The ones that get a name probably do.

---

## The Method: Belief → Wound → Refusal

Voice isn't vocabulary — an accent, a catchphrase, a verbal tic. Voice is what someone notices, what they ignore, what they defend, what they resent, and what they cannot stop talking about. You don't invent a voice. You build the thing underneath it, and the voice generates itself.

**1. Give them something they're wrong about — not factually, personally.**

A voice needs an interpretation of reality they've committed themselves to. Think of it as a private invariant, the same shape as an engine invariant (`rules/invariants.md`) but held by a person instead of the simulator:

- *"Nobody helps you for free."* — so when someone genuinely helps them, they don't feel gratitude. They feel an undisclosed debt.
- *"People only turn cruel when they're afraid."* — so they keep trying to make monsters feel safe.
- *"Everything can be repaired."* — so they can't accept that some things should be allowed to end.

**2. Give that belief a wound — a contradiction between what they believe, what happened to them, and what they refuse to conclude.**

The refusal is the important part. It's not that they haven't processed the contradiction — it's that they've processed it into something that lets the belief survive.

*Worked example, illustrative only, not a canon character:* Mara believes promises are sacred. Her mother made one promise her entire childhood — *"I'll come back"* — and never did. The honest conclusion is *promises are meaningless.* Mara's actual conclusion is *"then my mother wasn't strong enough."* The belief survives. The wound is what it cost to keep it standing.

Once that's in place, dialogue writes itself. Put someone in front of Mara who says *"sometimes you have to break a promise,"* and she doesn't hear an opinion. She hears an accusation against her entire understanding of reality. That reaction — not a speech pattern — is the voice.

**3. Ask what they'd actually say, not what they'd do — specifically when the belief is challenged.**

Not *"the Wastes are dangerous."* That's a fact, and facts don't have voices. *"The Wastes don't kill people. People kill themselves trying to make the Wastes mean something."* That's somebody talking. Put a second person from the same place next to them — *"My brother crossed them three times. Don't tell me they're cursed. He was just stupid."* — and a third — *"They're not cursed. They're grieving."* Now the location has competing interpretations instead of one description, and that's the actual difference between a lens with nobody looking through it and one with eyes behind it.

## Seats as a Voice Engine, Not a Character Sheet

Don't make a character *represent* a Seat. Give them a *relationship* to one. The same metaphysical fact, refracted through four unrelated lives, produces four voices for free:

- Devoted: *"Death is the only promise the world has never broken."*
- Afraid: *"Death doesn't take people. People hand them over."*
- Grateful, in a way that costs something to say out loud: *"My father died so I could live. Don't tell me Death is evil."*
- Emptied out by it: *"Don't romanticize it. Death is hungry."*

Same Seat. Four human beings. This works for any Seat, and it works better than trying to write "a Death person" from scratch — the domain is the constant, the life underneath it is what actually varies.

## Covering a Location

**Every major location should hold people who disagree about what it means.** Not factions — a faction tells you what side someone is on. A voice tells you why they think the side exists at all.

A useful minimum: **one person who loves the place, one who hates it, and one who refuses to interpret it.**

That third one matters most and gets skipped most often. This world is full of people who would rather live than understand — the scholar asks what the Unheld means; the sailor says *"I don't know what it means. I know which wind means we turn around."* The sailor isn't less developed than the scholar. Refusing to interpret is its own defended position, and it needs the same belief-and-wound treatment as anyone who has a theory.

## What This Doesn't Override

Some characters are thin on purpose, and this tool isn't a mandate to fix them. Weck's opacity is the character (`characters/weck/README.md` — a candidate exemplar precisely because nothing about him is explained, only enacted). Moth's file states outright that his want isn't a gap to fill, it's the design. Steve is written to actively resist interiority as a joke (`characters/steve-and-pip/README.md`). A flat antagonist can be a deliberate choice, not an oversight (Cole, `quests/sour-tomatoes.md`).

The question before applying this is the same redefinition-test instinct as everywhere else in this repo: is the thinness a gap, or is it load-bearing? If a file already tells you the ambiguity is the point, this tool has nothing to add.
