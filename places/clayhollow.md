# Clayhollow

A village in a misty valley ringed by low, weathered hills — nothing dramatic, just enough to feel sheltered and cut off. Stone cottages with slate roofs cluster around a central square that is always empty except for the slow, deliberate shuffle of clay golems. The people themselves haven't been seen in public in living memory. Everything is handled through the mail.

A traveler arriving is met by a golem at the gate with a sealed envelope: *"Welcome. State your needs in writing. Three days."*

No faces. No handshakes. Just ink and clay, and the faint sound of distant doors closing softly.

---

## The Golems

Roughly person-sized. Featureless faces with hollow eye sockets that seem to watch without seeing. Arms long enough to carry baskets or sacks without strain. Each household has one or two, named with short practical tags — Red-7, Moss-Hand, Pale-Thumb.

They don't speak. They deliver, stand guard at night, and return home at dusk. The villagers shape new ones every few years when the old crack beyond repair.

Repaired golems are considered more trustworthy. The faint color variations in the clay — new red pressed into old — are read like handwriting: *"That one's been mended three times — steady as stone."*

When a golem finally becomes too fragile to mend, it is carried to the cemetery by its household's newest golem and left standing as a marker. No grave, no name. Just a weathered figure slowly returning to the earth it came from.

---

## Cultural Rules

**Silence as courtesy.** Speaking aloud in public feels rude, like shouting in a library. Conversations happen on paper or not at all. People write long careful letters even to neighbors three doors down.

**The post is sacred.** Tampering with mail is the only real crime — punishable by exile. Exile means your golem stops receiving clay rations. You slowly starve in isolation.

**Your face is your most private possession.** Children are taught never to describe anyone else's appearance. Mirrors are small and kept hidden. People recognize each other by handwriting, the particular shade of ink they favor, or the way their golem walks — a slight limp from a poorly mended leg becomes a signature.

**Delayed emotions.** A love letter might take weeks to compose, be carried by golem, read in solitude, answered days later. Arguments simmer in ink for months. Joy and grief arrive second-hand and aged. This creates people who are extraordinarily patient but strangely detached.

**Rituals of indirect care.** If someone falls ill, neighbors send soup via golem with a note: *"Saw your light late last night — rest."* No visits. No hands on foreheads. Funerals are silent: the deceased's golem carries the body to the hill cemetery at dawn, leaves a single clay tablet with the name, then returns home empty-handed and stays there, slowly weathering into a marker.

## The Flower-Gathering Morning

The village's one quiet rebellion against its own rules. Everyone pretends not to notice it.

Around age five or six, a child is deemed old enough to "help the house." The parent writes a short note — always the same template — and seals it inside the golem's chest cavity:

> *Moss-Hand — Take [name] to the upper meadow. Let them gather what they will. Return by the long shadow. Do not let harm come.*

The golem bends at the waist so the child can climb onto its shoulder. They walk out through the back door — always the back — across dew-wet grass, over rabbit holes and around thistle patches, to a meadow wild with oxeye daisies and clover and the purple spikes of betony.

The golem stands motionless, basket held out, while the child fills it slowly: one flower for the table, one for the windowsill, one tucked behind their own ear and quickly hidden again before heading home. Sometimes the child talks to the golem anyway — soft one-sided chatter about cloud shapes, a beetle they found, how the wind feels. The golem never responds, but its head tilts fractionally, and the cracks in its clay catch the light differently.

Adults never speak of these outings. If a letter arrives asking "Did you see my little one in the meadow today?" the reply is always polite deflection: *"The golem returned with flowers. The house is brighter."*

The golems come back dusted with pollen, petals caught in their joints. Every parent keeps the old note tucked away in a drawer.

---

## The Golems as Confidants

People talk to their golems in whispers at home — things they would never write down. Some believe the golems remember. The cracks in the clay are called "scars" from carrying heavy secrets.

A few elderly villagers have begun leaving small offerings in their golem's hollow eye sockets: a polished stone, a feather, a dried petal. No one calls it prayer. No one calls it anything.

---

## Golem Repair

Repair is not a chore — it's a seasonal rite performed in anonymity.

When a golem's wear becomes undeniable, the owner writes a single unsigned note and places it inside the chest panel with: a fist-sized lump of fresh valley clay, a few tools, and a personal token — a dried petal, a polished stone, a scrap from an old shirt. The golem carries this bundle to the **Mending Hollow**, a shallow alcove behind the cemetery screened by thorn bushes, and stands motionless until morning.

By first light the bundle is gone and the golem returned — cracks filled, smoothed, refired in the mender's hidden kiln. The mender works alone in darkness, wearing linen gloves so no fingerprint is left. The personal token is buried inside the new clay. No credit claimed. No thank-you letters sent.

Children are taught to pack the bundle themselves by age eight or nine. They press a tiny fingerprint into the clay lump before sealing it — their first anonymous contribution to the village's life. The print disappears under the mender's smoothing. The child knows it was there.

Families pass down specific smoothing stones or paddles wrapped in cloth. The stone may have a tiny notch from generations of use. Holding it feels like touching the hands that came before, even if those hands were never seen.

After a repair, people often leave extra kindling or a jar of honey on their doorstep the following week — no note, no explanation. The recipient never asks who left it.

---

## The Before Time

Stories passed down in letters say there was a time when people spoke face-to-face and the valley rang with voices. Now they whisper it was a pact with the clay itself — let the earth carry our burdens so humans could rest.

No one knows. The oldest letters are too faded to read.

---

## What Grows Here

A quiet complicated affection laced with profound loneliness. Trust in the system — the golems never lie, never gossip — and suspicion of the humans behind it. A beautifully written letter can make someone fall in love with a stranger. A curt one can spark years of cold silence.

Most people feel a gentle pride in how well the system works, and a nagging sense that something vital slipped away generations ago.

---

## Open Hooks

**The unsigned letter.** A traveler's letter arrives with no name. The golem still delivered it. Someone wrote it. The village has no system for this.

**The child who doesn't stop.** One child starts leaving their own unsigned notes inside the golem's chest before each outing — not to the golem, but to whoever finds it at the Mending Hollow. A correspondence begins between a child and a stranger they will never see.

**The oldest letters.** Too faded to read, kept anyway. Someone has been trying to restore them with careful chemicals and lamplight. What they find changes what the Before Time means.

**The golem that came back wrong.** Returned from the Mending Hollow repaired, but carrying someone else's token inside it — a token that matches no family in the village.

**What exile actually looks like.** Someone was exiled within living memory. Their golem still stands at the edge of the cemetery — never weathered into a marker, still upright, still waiting. Their door is sealed. No one writes their name.
